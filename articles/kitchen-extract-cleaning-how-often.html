Commercial kitchens — Extract cleaning

How often should a kitchen extract system be cleaned?

By Raphael Whitmore TIFSM · 10 October 2026

No government guide sets cleaning intervals for kitchen extract systems. The 3, 6 and 12 month figures you will hear come from an industry guide. This article explains where they come from and how to work out which one applies to your kitchen.

What the government guidance says

The Home Office guide Fire safety risk assessment: small and medium places of assembly lists “cooking equipment, hot ducting, flues and filters” as sources of ignition.1 Among the common causes of equipment fires it names “allowing extraction equipment in catering environments to build up excessive grease deposits”.1 It also says: “Ensure cooking and catering equipment is installed, used, maintained and protected in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.”1

It gives no cleaning interval. Its own preface says: “It does not set prescriptive standards, but provides recommendations and guidance for use when assessing the adequacy of fire precautions”.1 It is written for places such as pubs, clubs and village halls, not cafés in particular, but the point about grease applies to any catering kitchen.

London Fire Brigade says: “Kitchen ducting should be cleaned regularly to avoid a build-up of grease.” It adds that the grease “could ignite and cause smoke and fire to spread to other areas of the property and nearby premises.”2

Where the 3, 6 and 12 month figures come from

They come from TR/19 Grease, a guide published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It sets minimum cleaning intervals by how many hours a day the kitchen cooks.4

Level of use Hours cooking per day Clean every
Heavy 12 to 16 hours 3 months
Moderate 6 to 12 hours 6 months
Light 2 to 6 hours 12 months

The table is as BESA publishes it on its website. TR/19 itself is a paid document, so check the guide for the full detail. Frying, wood and charcoal cooking can push a kitchen into a heavier band. The London Fire Brigade guide says: “Many extraction systems will need a higher frequency of cleaning based on hours in use and the type of usage.”3

The 200 micron limit

The intervals exist to stop grease building up past a limit. BESA says the grease between cleans must not exceed a mean average of 200 microns (0.2 mm). It describes that as “approximately half the thickness of an average business card”. It also says that “Areas showing grease deposits of 500 microns or more require immediate spot cleaning attention.”4

The thickness is measured with a gauge, such as a wet film thickness gauge, which is pressed into the grease. Ask your contractor for readings before and after each clean.

London Fire Brigade's annual minimum

London Fire Brigade's guidance note GN86, Catering Kitchen Extract Systems, sets a floor: “All catering kitchen extract systems systems will require annual cleaning as a minimum, unless the fire risk assessment recommends otherwise.”3 It also says: “The frequency of cleaning should be such that grease deposit limits are not exceeded.”3

That makes annual cleaning the least a kitchen should do, not a target for a busy one. Canopies and filters are different: GN86 says “Periodic specialist cleaning should be accompanied by daily or weekly cleaning of canopies and filters”, usually by the kitchen operator.3 GN86 is written for London, but it is a good free fire service document on the subject.

Work out your level

  1. How many hours a day does the kitchen cook? That puts you in a TR/19 band.
  2. Do you do a lot of frying, chargrilling, wood or charcoal cooking? If so, you may need to go up a band.
  3. When was the extract system last cleaned, and did you get a certificate?
  4. Does your buildings insurer set any cleaning requirements?

Your fire risk assessment should cover the extract system. Kitchen extract systems should be covered by the fire risk assessment required under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005.
