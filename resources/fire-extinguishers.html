Reference notes — Fire extinguishers

Fire extinguishers: what the law and the standards say

By Raphael Whitmore TIFSM · 12 October 2026

Working notes on portable fire extinguishers in England: the law, how many, how far apart, servicing and the foam ban. The British Standards that set the numbers are paid documents, so the figures here come from the NSI bulletin on BS 5306-8:2023, BAFE, the Fire Industry Association and fire and rescue service guidance. The last section lists what has not been checked.

The short answer

  • The law gives no numbers. The Fire Safety Order requires appropriate equipment, kept maintained. The fire risk assessment decides what that means.1
  • The numbers come from BS 5306-8:2023 (selection and siting) and BS 5306-3 (commissioning and maintenance). They are guidance, not law, but assessors, insurers and extinguisher companies work to them.4
  • Servicing: a visual check at least monthly, a basic service by a competent person every 12 months, and an extended service every 5 years (water, foam, powder) or 10 years (CO₂ and sealed powder).5
  • Foam: PFOA-containing AFFF extinguishers have been banned across the UK since 4 July 2025.

The law

Article 13(1) of the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 says that where necessary, premises must be equipped with “appropriate fire-fighting equipment” and any non-automatic equipment must be “easily accessible, simple to use and indicated by signs.” What is appropriate depends on the size and use of the premises, the equipment and substances present, and the number of people (13(2)). The responsible person must also nominate competent people to deal with firefighting (13(3)).1

Article 17 requires the premises and equipment to be kept under a suitable maintenance system and in good working order.1

What the government guides say

The guide for small non-domestic premises says: “It is recommended that a means of ‘first aid’ firefighting is provided.” A water-based extinguisher for combustible materials and a carbon dioxide extinguisher for electrical fires, on each floor, is considered adequate for a typical premises. Where there is a fat fryer, it recommends a fire blanket and a class F extinguisher. It asks for a monthly visual check, an annual inspection and test by a competent contractor, and a log.2

The Home Office guide for small and medium places of assembly says that in simple premises “one or two portable extinguishers of the appropriate type, readily available for use” may be enough. Otherwise, extinguishers should be sited on the escape routes at each floor level, with hazard-specific ones close to the hazard. It also says people with no training should not be expected to fight a fire.3

The two guides differ on checks: the assembly guide says weekly, the small non-domestic guide says monthly. Treat monthly as the minimum and weekly walk-rounds as good practice.

How many: class A (wood, paper, textiles)

Floor area per storeyMinimum
Up to 50 m²1 extinguisher, 26A in total
50 to 400 m²2 or more, 26A combined
Over 400 m²2 or more, floor area × 0.065 = combined A rating needed

This is from the NSI bulletin on BS 5306-8:2023 (clause 7.2.1).4 A 9 litre water extinguisher is 13A, so two make 26A. The sum gives the combined rating, not the number of extinguishers: 500 m² × 0.065 = 32.5A, which three 13A extinguishers (39A) would meet.8 Some fire service pages say “two per floor” more simply. The bulletin is the reference for the 2023 edition.

The number in a rating is the length of the test fire. For A, it is the wood crib in decimetres, so 13A is about 1.3 m and 26A about 2.6 m. For B, it is litres of liquid. For F, it is litres of cooking oil: 5F, 25F, 40F and 75F.7

The first four rows and the 0.11 row match the Fire Industry Association’s F-class fact file (BS 5306-8:2023, clause 7.6.2, Table 2).7 The other rows are from my diploma course table only. Above 0.4 m², the course notes say an automatic fire suppression system is recommended; I haven’t found that in a free source. The NSI bulletin says Table 2 was amended in 2023 without giving the new values, so check the table against the standard before quoting it to a client.

Wet chemical is the usual medium, with a fire blanket for fryers. Never use water, CO₂, powder or ordinary foam on cooking oil.7

How many: class B (flammable liquids)

The number and type depend on the volume of flammable liquid present. A competent, trained person who knows the liquid needs to assess it, and the assessor should see that information on the visit and check the right extinguishers are in place and maintained. I haven’t found the Table 1 values in a free source.

This chart is built from the government guides, BAFE and the Fire Industry Association, not from the standard. Check the label and the manufacturer’s instructions. Classes C (gas) and D (metals) are left out. Class C extinguishers should not normally be provided, and class D is case by case with specialist advice.4

How far, and where

ClassMaximum travel distance
A30 m
B10 m
F10 m
Electrical30 m (general extinguishers), 10 m (dedicated)

Take 5 m off for every door that has to be opened on the way (BS 5306-8:2023, clause 8.4, via the NSI bulletin).4 At 30 m travel, class A extinguishers can be up to 60 m apart.9

  • Near exits, corridors and escape routes, so nobody goes further into the building to fetch one.
  • Beside call points. Special-risk extinguishers near the hazard, but reachable (at the boiler room door, not next to the boiler).9
  • Where someone can turn their back on the fire and escape if it fails. This is from my course teaching and I haven’t found it in a source.
  • The right type for the fire risk at that spot, with a sign where mixed types sit together.
  • Wall mounted, with the handle about 1.5 m high (up to 4 kg) or 1 m (heavier). This is from secondary sources, not the standard.
  • Protected from damage, theft and corrosion (clause 8.5).

Keep a log of every check, service and fault.2 The order does not say who services them; guidance says a competent person, ideally from a company certified under a third-party scheme, and the responsible person stays responsible for the contractor’s work.5 One trade article advises replacing any extinguisher after 20 years; I haven’t verified that.

P50 “service-free” extinguishers

Britannia P50 units, sold by Safelincs, are sealed extinguishers with a 10-year agent guarantee and no technician service. All of this comes from the seller, so treat it as the supplier’s claim.12

  • They still need an annual visual inspection by a trained member of staff, logged, and a regular walk-by (the supplier recommends monthly).
  • After 10 years they are swapped for a factory-refurbished unit.
  • The supplier says they meet BS 5306-3 and that insurers have approved them, naming none. I haven’t verified either. Tell the client to check with their insurer.
  • On site, record them as service-free units, check the annual inspection records and the 10-year date, and don’t mark them overdue just because there is no service label.

The foam ban

  • In force in the UK: since 4 July 2025, extinguishers containing PFOA (“C8” AFFF foam) can't be used, tested, refilled or maintained. Take them out of use and don’t refill. Dispose of them as hazardous waste through a licensed contractor.15
  • Who is at risk: older units, most likely made or refilled before about 2016. A label or safety data sheet mentioning PFOA, PFOS or “perfluorinated” is a warning sign.
  • Not all foam is banned. Many modern foam extinguishers remain compliant.16
  • Proposed, not law in England: a wider PFAS restriction. HSE consulted on it between August 2025 and February 2026 and it hasn’t been adopted. Check HSE for the current position.14
  • EU and Northern Ireland have set dates (no new AFFF extinguishers on the market from 23 October 2026). They don’t bind England, Scotland or Wales.13

Not checked yet

  • The text of BS 5306-8:2023, BS 5306-3 and BS EN 3-7. Everything from them is second-hand.
  • The class B provision table, the class F rows beyond the four matched, and whether my course table is from the 2023 edition.
  • Electrical travel distance: my course table says 10 m, the NSI bulletin says 30 m general or 10 m dedicated.
  • The 20-year maximum life, mounting heights, BS 5499 sign wording and BS 7863 colours.
  • The P50 claims on legality and insurers.
  • The PFAS timetable for England, and the exact EU end-of-use date (30 or 31 December 2030).
  • This is England only. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have different legislation.

On site: questions to ask

  1. What is the floor area of each storey?
  2. What hazards are there: cooking (and the oil surface area), flammable liquids (and the volume), electrical, battery charging?
  3. For flammable liquids, who assessed the provision, and can they show the information?
  4. Where are the extinguishers, how far apart, and are they visible and signed?
  5. Is the right type at each point? Could someone use one and still escape?
  6. When was the last annual service, and is there a log of monthly or weekly checks?
  7. Are there any P50 units? Who was trained, and where are the inspection records?
  8. Are there any foam extinguishers, and how old are they? Any labelled PFOA or PFAS?
  9. Who is trained to use them? Is the service company BAFE SP101 registered?
